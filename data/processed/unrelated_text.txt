Tea was first cultivated in southwestern China, where leaves of the Camellia sinensis shrub were boiled as a medicine long before they became an everyday drink. Over many centuries the habit spread along trade routes to Japan, Korea, Central Asia and eventually Europe, where Dutch and Portuguese merchants brought chests of dried leaves home by sea. Each region developed its own customs: whisked green powder in formal ceremonies, pressed bricks carried by caravans across mountain passes, and strong black brews softened with milk and sugar. Growers learned that altitude, rainfall and the timing of the harvest change the flavor of the leaf, and that withering, rolling and drying determine whether it becomes green, oolong or black tea.
